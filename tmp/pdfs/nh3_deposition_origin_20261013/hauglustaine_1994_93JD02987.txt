Abstract. An interactive two-dimensional model of the troposphere, stratosphere, and 
mesosphere, in which dynamics, radiation, and chemistry are treated interactively, is used 
to investigate the anthropogenic changes in the steady state chemical composition of the 
atmosphere since preindustrial times and to assess the associated changes in radiative 
forcing on climate. The perturbations in the atmospheric oxidation capacity due to 
anthropogenic emissions of source gases are found to be significant. In the troposphere, 
an ozone increase of 80-120% at northern midlatitudes and a global decrease of 10- 
20% in the OH concentration since the preindustrial period are calculated. In the polar 
lower stratosphere of the southern hemisphere, an ozone depletion since preindustrial 
times reaching more than 60% during spring is calculated as a result of rapid catalytical 
destruction of ozone by chlorine radicals in the presence of polar stratospheric clouds. 
Particular attention is given to the induced changes in radiative forcing. These results 
stress the potentially important role of chemical feedbacks on climate and indicate that the 
direct forcing associated with increasing concentrations of greenhouse gases is enhanced 
by about 30% when these feedbacks are taken into account. On a global average basis, 
the greenhouse effect of tropospheric ozone represents approximately 17% of the total 
radiative perturbation. This forcing is characterized by a strong latitudinal dependence, 
peaking at midlatitudes in the northern hemisphere. The importance of indirect climate 
forcings by stratospheric ozone (including local cooling of the stratosphere) is confirmed. 
It is found that the net (solar + infrared) indirect effect of stratospheric ozone changes is to 
increase the chlorofluorocarbon direct radiative forcing. On the other hand, the change in 
the longwave forcing associated with water vapor increase in the stratosphere appears to 
play a minor role. 
1. Introduction 
It is increasingly obvious on the basis of various records 
that human activities over the last decades have changed 
the chemical composition of the global atmosphere [World 
Meteorological Organization (WMO), 1988; 1991]. Indus- 
trial emissions, intensification of agriculture practises and 
the development of transportation have directly enhanced 
the atmospheric level of several gases such as carbon dioxide 
(CO2), nitrous oxide (N20), methane (CH4), carbon monox- 
ide (CO), nonmethane hydrocarbons (NMHCs), nitrogen ox- 
ides (NO), sulfur dioxide (SO2), and chlorofluorocarbons

processes from unpolluted to more polluted regions with in-

of most previous studies is that enhanced carbon monoxide 
and methane mixing ratios have induced an OH concentra- 
tion decrease since preindustrial times of about 10-30% on 
a global average, with a consequent increase of chemical 
lifetimes of CO, CH4, and several other species. 
The direct radiative forcing associated with increase of 
greenhouse gas concentrations in the atmosphere since prein- 
dustrial times has been estimated to be in the range 2- 
2.5 W m -2 with a 10-20% uncertainty owing to impreci- 
sions in the radiative parameters [Hansen and Lacis, 1990]. 
The direct radiative forcing is defined as the perturbation in 
the net radiative flux at the tropopause due to changes in 
concentrations of trace species, with water vapor changes 
and chemical interactions being excluded [Ramanathan et 
al., 1987; IPCC, 1990]. Limited work has been devoted to 
the indirect climatic perturbations. However, as illustrated 
by Lelieveld and Crutzen [ 1992] in the case of methane, by 
Ramaswamy et al. [ 1992] and Schwarzkopf and Ramaswamy 
[1993] in the case of stratospheric ozone and by Wang et al. 
[ 1993] in the case of tropospheric ozone, the chemistry feed- 
backs on climate may represent an important part of the total 
radiative forcing. 
As far as the global atmospheric composition evolution 
is concerned, it seems necessary to take into account both 
the dynamical and photochemical interactions between the 
troposphere and the stratosphere. Concerning photochemi- 
cal interactions, a deeper penetration of ultraviolet radiation 
when stratospheric ozone is depleted may play an important 
role on tropospheric ozone by increasing its photodissoci- 
ation [Thompson et al., 1990) and thereby enhancing OH 
concentrations [Madronich and Granier, 1992]. 
The purpose of this paper is to calculate both direct and 
chemically induced radiative forcings on climate resulting 
from atmospheric composition changes since preindustrial 
times (pristine Holocene). A 2-D model of the atmosphere 
extending from the surface to 85 km in which chemical, ra- 
diative, and dynamical processes are treated interactively is 
used in this work. This model includes a detailed radia- 
tive code which derives the diabatic heating rates consis- 
tently with calculated distributions of temperature and trace 
species. The tropospheric chemistry takes into account the 
methane oxidation scheme and includes a realistic latitudinal 
representation of both natural and anthropogenic emissions 
of the major source gases. The scheme for stratospheric 
chemistry includes a simplified treatment of heterogeneous 
processes in polar stratospheric clouds (PSCs). This model is 
described in more detail in section 2. The simulated changes 
in the steady state atmospheric composition since the prein- 
dustrial era are discussed in section 3 and the corresponding 
radiative perturbations are described in section 4. Attention 
is mainly focused on the tropospheric chemistry processes 
and on the chemically induced radiative forcing. The role 
played by stratospheric ozone depletion on the radiative bud- 
get is also examined. 
2. Model description 
The model used in this study is a coupled chemical dy- 
namical radiative 2-D model extending from 85øS to 85øN 
with a latitudinal resolution of 5 ø, and rom 0 to 85 km with 
a vertical resolution of 1 km. The original model has been 
developed by Brasseur et al. [1990a] to study the middle 
atmosphere. Its current version has been extended down to 
the surface and is described in more detail by Hauglustaine 
[1992]. 
2.1 Chemistry 
The model includes about 60 chemical species and 130 
chemical/photochemical reactions. The concentrations of 
CO, CO2, CH4, CH302H, N20, HNO3, N205, H, HF, 
H20, H2, H202, HCN, CH3CN, CC14, CFC13 (CFC 11), 
CF2C12 (CFC 12), CH3CC13, CH3C1, CHC1F2 (HCFC 22), 
CC12FCC1F2 (CFC 113), CC1F2CC1F2 (CFC 114), CC1F2CF3 
(CFC 115), CF2BrC1 (Ha 1211), CF3Br (Ha 1301), CC120, 
CC1FO, CF20 and CH3Br are calculated by solving a con- 
tinuity-transport equation for each species. Short-lived 
species are grouped into photochemical families: O = O(3p) 
+ 03 + O(1D), NOy = N + NO + NO2 + NO3 + HO2NO2 + 
CIONO2 + HNO3 + 2 N205, CI = CI + C10 + HOC1 + HC1 + 
C1ONO2 + OC10 + 2 C1202 and Br = Br + BrO + BrONO2 
+ HBr + HOBr for which a continuity-transport equation is 
solved. Concentrations of OH, HO2, CH20 and CH302 are 
calculated assuming photochemical equilibrium conditions. 
The representation of the tropospheric chemistry adopted in 
the original model has been improved by the addition of a 
methane oxidation scheme with an explicit calculation of 
CH20, CH3, CH30, CH302 or CH302H. On the other hand, 
the chemistry of NMHCs and peroxyacetyl nitrate (PAN) 
is not considered in the present version of the model. The 
reaction rate constants included in the model are based on 
the JPL 19913 compilation [DeMore et al., 1990] except for 
the reactions of CH4 and CH3CC13 with hydroxyl radical 
where the values from Vaghjiani and Ravishankara [1991] 
and DeMore [ 1992] are respectively adopted. The photodis- 
sociation rates are calculated from the solar irradiances given 
by Brasseur and Simon [ 1981 ] and from the absorption cross 
sections compiled by DeMore et al. [ 1990]. Photolysis rates 
are modified in the troposphere in the presence of clouds 
following the formalism proposed by Chang et al. [1987]. 
The cloud cover is specified according to the monthly-mean 
ISCCP climatology [Schiffer and Rossow, 1983] and corre- 
sponds to a latitudinal-seasonal dependent three-layer cover. 
A constant liquid water path of 40 gm-2 consistent with cur- 
rent estimates [Slingo and Slingo, 1991] has been adopted as 
a working value, providing a cloud optical depth of about 6. 
Eddy diffusivities (Kyu and K**) are parameterized rela- 
tive to the mean dynamical state within the stratosphere and 
mesosphere. The effect of planetary wave absorption is for- 
mulated according to Hitchman and Brasseur [1988], while 
that of gravity wave breaking is parameterized according to 
Lindzen [ 1981 ]. In the troposphere, the coefficients given by 
Plumb andMahlman [ 1987] are adopted. Note that ChatfieM 
and Crutzen [ 1984] have stressed the importance of vertical 
convective transport in the troposphere and, hence, the inad- 
equacy of eddy diffusion parameterizations to fully simulate 
vertical exchanges below the tropopause. The fact that this 
kind of process is not included in this simplified 2-D model 
is an important limitation to bear in mind.

level as a function of the local temperature and pressure

2.2 Climate 
The dynamical fields (, *, *, O) are calculated in- 
teractively in the whole atmosphere. However, the stream 
function is calculated with a lower boundary condition spec- 
ified at 3 km in order to avoid the problems associated 
with the parameterization of complex exchange processes 
in the planetary boundary layer. The energy, mass and zonal 
momentum conservation equations are expressed, using the 
transformed Eulerian mean meridional circulation formalism 
in log-pressure coordinates [Andrews and Mcintyre, 1976]. 
Energy and momentum deposition by gravity and Rossby 
wave dissipation are calculated interactively from the para- 
meterizations described by Brasseur and Hitchman [1987] 
and Hitchman and Brasseur [1988]. The contribution of 
tropospheric synoptic waves is taken into account through a 
prescribed Eliassen-Palm flux divergence derived from An- 
drews et al. [1983]. In the lowest model level, a friction 
term is added following the classical Ekman layer formu- 
lation with a drag coefficient of 100 m2/s [Holton, 1979]. 
As in Garcia et al. [1992], an idealized latent heat release 
associated with deep convection in the troposphere is intro- 
duced in tropical regions. This heating reaches a maximum 
of 1.3 K/day in equatorial upper troposphere and decreases 
exponentially towards the pole. 
The radiative net heating rate is computed from the band 
model described by Kiehl et al. [ 1987], Kiehl and Briegleb 
[1991] and Briegleb [1992] and includes the radiative con- 
tributions of water vapor, carbon dioxide, ozone, methane, 
nitrous oxide, CFC 11 and CFC 12. 
The atmospheric model is coupled to a simplified ver- 
sion of the surface energy balance model (EBM) described 
by Hauglustaine and Gdrard [1991]. This surface model 
includes a land-ocean partitioning. In each latitudinal bin, 
the temperature of both land and oceanic mixed layer are 
computed using thermodynamic equation. The meridional 
oceanic heat flux is proportional to the meridional tempera- 
tui'e gradient with coefficients derived from observations and 
depending on ocean and sea-ice fractional covers. The sur- 
face albedo is calculated taking into account the land-snow 
and sea-ice covers. These fractional covers are obtained from 
empirical temperature dependent parameterizations [Robock, 
1983] (see Hauglustaine and Gtrard for more details). The 
value of the surface albedo affects the radiative code while 
that of sea-ice and snow covers are used to determine the 
surface dry deposition velocities. The surface temperature is 
used as a boundary condition for the resolution of the atmo- 
spheric thermodynamic equation. The temperature profile 
is adjusted in the troposphere using the classical convective 
adjustment scheme of Manabe and Wetherald [ 1967].

emissions of the major source gases are considered on an 
annual basis with a relative contribution and latitudinal dis-

[ 1991 ] for CH4 and on Hough [ 1991 ] for other species (i.e., 
N20, NO, CO, H2).

5. Conclusions 
The purpose of this paper was to investigate the changes 
in the steady state chemical composition of the atmosphere 
since preindustrial times and to assess the associated radia- 
tive forcing on climate. The model adopted for this work 
is an interactive chemical-dynamical-radiative 2-D dimen- 
sional model of the troposphere, stratosphere and mesosphere 
including a realistic latitudinal representation of both natu- 
ral and anthropogenic emissions of the major source gases, 
a simplified treatment of heterogeneous processes on PSC 
particules. The model is coupled to a surface energy balance 
model. 
The validity of the model results remains limited by sim- 
plifications and omissions in the formulation of several pro- 
cesses. For exemple, a realistic simulation of short-lived 
species with highly localized surface emissions such as ni- 
trogen oxides should be performed by 3-D model including 
an adequate representation of convective vertical transport. 
Furthermore, the chemical scheme used in this study does 
not include NMHC species which are believed to contribute 
up to 15% to the production of tropospheric ozone [Hough 
and Johnson, 1991; Law and Pyle, 1991 ]. It should also be 
noted that the role of clouds in tropospheric chemistry is ne- 
glected in this model. However, as shown by Lelieveld and 
Crutzen [1991], photochemical processes in the lower half 
of the troposphere could be strongly affected by the presence 
of liquid water clouds. This kind of climate-chemistry in- 
teraction study should be refined in the near future with the 
emergence of coupled 3-D climate-chemistry models. 
Despite these uncertainties and limitations, interesting 
characteristics of chemically induced radiative forcings on 
climate have been illustrated and the important role played 
by chemical feedbacks has been pointed out. Our results 
indicate that the direct radiative forcing associated with in- 
creases in greenhouse gas concentration since the preindus- 
trial period is enhanced by about 30% when these feedbacks 
are taken into account. The increased tropospheric ozone 
longwave radiation trapping represents about 17% of the to- 
tal radiative perturbation on a global average. This infrared 
forcing varies strongly with latitude and peaks in the northern 
hemisphere midlatitudes where anthropogenic emissions of 
ozone precursors reach their maximum. The important role 
played by the indirect forcing of ozone through local temper- 
ature adjustment in the lower polar stratosphere is confirmed. 
The net effect of indirect forcings is to increase the the direct 
CFC forcing. Furthermore, our results suggest that the con- 
tribution to climate forcing of enhanced stratospheric water 
vapor (resulting from methane oxidation) to climate forcing 
is minor.
